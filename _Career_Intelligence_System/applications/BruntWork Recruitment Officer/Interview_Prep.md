# Interview Prep: Recruitment Officer, BruntWork

Short, sharp, first person. Learn the idea behind each answer and say it in your own words.

## What to expect

- **Application:** BruntWork's recruiter listings ask for the Recruiter Assessment, assessment questions, a technical check and a voice recording. Complete everything in one sitting; complete applications are reviewed first, and pay is set partly by how well you do here.
- **Technical check:** they test your internet speed and computer. Run a speed test beforehand, close other apps, and have your power backup ready.
- **Voice recording:** usually a short introduction. Use the 60-second intro below. Quiet room, headset, smile while you speak.
- **Screening call:** often 15 to 20 minutes by phone or video. Expect "tell me about yourself", "why recruitment", shift and contractor questions.
- **Practical tests:** offshore recruiter interviews increasingly test skills live: build a Boolean search, write an outreach message, explain how you'd handle a candidate who goes silent. Practise the drills at the end of this pack.
- **Watch-outs from candidate reviews:** follow-up can be slow. Reply fast, stay polite, and send one short follow-up after 3 to 4 working days.

## The full recruitment cycle

### Walk me through the full recruitment cycle.

> It starts with a clear intake from the client: must-haves, pay, shift and culture. Then I write the ad and source through applicants, LinkedIn and referrals. I screen for skills and fit, including pay, shift and setup, run assessments, and endorse my top two or three with clear reasons. I coordinate the client interviews, secure a firm yes at offer, and support onboarding. After the start, I check in so the hire actually lasts. Throughout, every candidate hears back from me, and their data is handled properly.

BruntWork's own job ad is the middle of this cycle: "source, screen, facilitate hiring, secure clear commitments, positive candidate experience, data privacy."

### The 11 stages in detail

| Stage | What happens | What you say you'd do |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Intake | Client explains the role | Must-haves, nice-to-haves, pay, shift, time zone, culture, deadline |
| 2. Job ad | Advert written and posted | Clear on the work, pay, shift and growth; careers site, job boards, LinkedIn |
| 3. Sourcing | Find candidates | Applicants, LinkedIn Boolean searches, talent pool, referrals |
| 4. Screening | Check CVs, short call | Skills against must-haves, pay, shift, internet and power, start date |
| 5. Assessment | Prove the skills | Skills tests, technical checks, voice recordings |
| 6. Shortlist | Endorse to the client | Top two or three with a short email on why (your assessment task) |
| 7. Client interviews | Client meets candidates | Schedule, prepare both sides, collect feedback fast |
| 8. Selection and checks | Final choice | References and identity confirmed |
| 9. Offer and commitment | Close the hire | Present the offer, handle counteroffers, get a clear yes |
| 10. Onboarding | Get them started | Contract, start date, setup, first-week check-in |
| 11. Follow-up and close-out | Make it last | 30, 60 and 90-day check-ins; replace if needed; tell unsuccessful candidates; update the ATS; delete data no longer needed |

### Which stage matters most?

Intake. A vague brief wastes every stage after it. Ten minutes of sharp questions with the client saves weeks of the wrong candidates.

### Where do hires usually fall apart?

At the offer, when pay, shift or start date weren't confirmed early. That's why I settle those at screening, so the offer is a formality, not a surprise.

## Sourcing and attracting talent

### How do you attract the right talent?

I go where they are: LinkedIn, job boards, tech communities, referrals and job fairs. My job ads are clear about the work, pay and shift, and they show the culture and growth. With IT people, I speak their language, which earns trust fast.

### Where would you source a remote IT support specialist?

LinkedIn first, with a Boolean search. Then job boards, tech groups on Facebook, Telegram and WhatsApp, and referrals from good candidates I've already spoken to. Strong people know other strong people.

### How do you write an outreach message that doesn't feel like a template?

Short, personal and specific. One line about something real in their profile, one line on the role, pay and shift, and one clear ask: a 15-minute call. No walls of text.

### How do you source for a hard-to-fill role?

Widen the net carefully: similar job titles, adjacent industries, people one step below who are ready to move up. I ask the client which must-haves are truly must-haves. And I ask strong candidates for referrals.

### How do you sell a role to a passive candidate?

I find out what they want first: growth, pay, flexibility, better work. Then I show how the role gives it, honestly. If it doesn't fit them, I say so and keep the relationship for the next one.

### How would you build a pipeline before a role opens?

Keep a shortlist of good candidates per common role, such as IT support, virtual assistant and customer service. Tag them in the ATS, stay in touch, and when a role opens, call them first.

## Screening and assessment

### How would you screen an IT candidate?

I ask them to walk me through a real problem they fixed: what broke, what they checked and why. Real experience gives specifics; weak candidates stay general. Then I check the client's must-have tools, pay, shift and start date.

### How would you screen a role you know less about, say bookkeeping?

I'd ask the client three things: must-haves, nice-to-haves and deal-breakers. I'd study profiles of people who do that job well and learn the key terms. Then I'd use the same method: ask for real examples and listen for specifics.

### What do you always cover in a screening call?

Motivation, must-have skills, pay expectations, shift and time zone, internet and power setup, start date, other offers in progress, and contractor status. Logistics found early save weeks later.

### What red flags do you listen for?

Vague answers about their own work, blaming every past employer, pay or shift expectations far from the role, and someone who can't explain a tool they listed.

### How do you check a remote candidate is genuine?

Camera on. I confirm their identity and ask unscripted follow-up questions on their CV. If someone pauses, reads or can't explain their own work, I dig deeper. Proxy interviews are a real risk in remote hiring.

### How do you handle a gap in a CV?

I ask neutrally and move on. What matters is whether their skills are current and whether they can do the job now.

### A candidate looks overqualified. Is that a problem?

Not by itself. I check why they want this role, whether the pay works for them, and whether they'll stay. If the answers are solid, they're often a great hire.

### How do you assess culture fit without bias?

I define fit as work behaviours: reliability, communication, responsiveness and ownership. I ask every candidate the same questions and score against the same criteria. "Someone like me" is not culture fit.

### What questions shouldn't you ask in an interview?

Anything about age, marital status, pregnancy or children, religion, tribe or ethnicity, health or disability, or politics. In some US states, salary history too. I stick to what predicts performance.

### A candidate lied on their CV. What do you do?

I end it politely, note the facts in the ATS, and don't submit them. Clients trust our shortlist; one fake candidate costs that trust.

## Candidate experience and offers

### What does a good candidate experience mean to you?

Fast replies, clear next steps, honest feedback and no silence. Every candidate should know where they stand, even if the answer is no.

### A candidate goes silent mid-process. What do you do?

A set sequence. Day 1: email and a WhatsApp or LinkedIn message. Day 2: a call. Day 4: a final note: "I'll close your file on Friday unless I hear from you." Then I close it and move to my backup. I always keep two or three backups warm.

### Why do candidates ghost, and how do you prevent it?

Usually slow process, silence from us, or a faster offer elsewhere. Prevention: regular updates, even "no news yet, next update Friday", and asking early what else they're interviewing for.

### How do you secure a candidate's commitment to an offer?

No surprises. I confirm pay, shift and expectations at screening, keep in touch through the process, and ask directly before the offer: "If it comes in at this, will you accept?" A clear yes before the offer beats a maybe after it.

### A candidate wants more than the client's budget.

I find out early, not at the offer. If the gap is small, I check what's flexible: a pay review after 90 days, incentives or start date. If it's big, I'm honest with both sides. If the market rate is above the budget, I show the client the data.

### A candidate accepts, then backs out before starting.

I find out why, tell the client immediately with a replacement plan, and go straight to my backups. Then I fix the cause: usually a counteroffer or a doubt I missed at screening.

### How do you handle a counteroffer from their current employer?

I raise it early: "If your employer counters, what will you do?" I remind them why they started looking. A counteroffer rarely fixes the reason they wanted to leave.

### How do you reject many candidates without it feeling cold?

Quick and personal. One line of thanks, one line of reason, and one line of encouragement. Under 90 seconds each, but never a silent no.

## Clients and hiring managers

### How do you work with angry clients?

I stay calm and let them finish. I acknowledge the problem, get the facts, agree a fix and a time, and follow up before they chase me. Angry clients usually just want to know someone owns it.

### What do you ask a client in an intake call?

Must-haves, nice-to-haves and deal-breakers. Pay, shift and time zone. What success looks like at 90 days. Who interviews and how fast they decide.

### A client keeps rejecting strong candidates without clear reasons.

I ask for specific feedback on each one, compare it against the brief, and look for the pattern. Then I recalibrate with the client using real profiles: "Here are three you rejected. What's missing?" Usually the brief changes.

### A client wants to skip steps for a candidate they like.

I respect it, but keep the minimum checks: skills, references and privacy consent. I explain the risk in one sentence and let them decide with the facts.

### How do you build trust with a client who's been let down before?

Small promises, kept fast. A strong shortlist on time, honest updates, and no padding with weak candidates.

## Targets, volume and metrics

### How do you manage many open roles at once?

One tracker, ranked by urgency and how close each role is to filling. I block my shift: sourcing first, screens in the middle, updates and admin at the end. Every role gets a touch every day.

### You're behind target halfway through the month.

I look at the funnel to find the leak: not enough candidates, too few passing screens, or clients not converting. Then I fix that stage: more sourcing hours, tighter screening, or faster client feedback. I also revisit strong runners-up from earlier roles.

### Which recruitment metrics matter?

Time to fill, submissions per hire, interview-to-offer rate, offer acceptance rate, and how long hires stay. They show where the process is slow or leaking.

### How do you calculate time to fill?

Days from the day the role opens to the day the offer is accepted. I'd learn BruntWork's benchmark per role and aim to beat it.

### How do you keep ATS data clean when you're busy?

Update as I go, not at the end of the day. Every call ends with a stage change and a next step in the system. If it's not in the ATS, it didn't happen.

### Have you used an ATS?

I've run pipelines in Freshdesk and Zoho Desk: every request logged, tracked by stage and closed on time. An ATS sits in the same family, and I learn new tools fast.

### How do you use AI tools in recruiting?

To speed up the first draft: job ads, outreach and Boolean ideas. I always check and personalise before sending, and I never paste candidate personal data into public AI tools.

## Data privacy

### How do you protect candidate data?

Only collect what the role needs, store it in the ATS rather than personal files or chats, share CVs with clients only with the candidate's consent, and delete what's no longer needed. I work with confidential patient data every day, so this is habit for me.

### Which privacy laws apply to this work?

It depends on where the candidate and client are: Kenya's Data Protection Act, the Philippines' Data Privacy Act, GDPR for UK and EU clients. The principles are the same: consent, minimum data, secure storage and limited access.

## Practical drills

### Boolean search: remote IT support specialist

```
("IT support" OR "help desk" OR "service desk" OR "desktop support")
AND ("Microsoft 365" OR "Office 365" OR "Active Directory" OR Intune)
AND (remote OR "work from home")
NOT (manager OR director OR intern)
```

Explain it: titles in the first bracket, must-have tools in the second, remote in the third, and NOT removes seniority that won't fit. Add a location if the client needs one.

### Boolean search: Microsoft 365 administrator

```
("Microsoft 365" OR "Office 365" OR M365) AND (administrator OR admin OR engineer)
AND (Exchange OR Intune OR Entra OR "Azure AD")
NOT (sales OR recruiter)
```
