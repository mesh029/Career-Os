## 2. Unrealistic timelines and budgets

### How do you handle a client with an unrealistic timeline or budget?

> I handle it in 3 steps: **Acknowledge, Show data, Give options.**
>
> 1. **Acknowledge, don't argue.** "I understand you need this urgently and you have a tight budget."
> 2. **Show data, not opinion.**
> - *Timeline:* "For this kind of IT Support role, our average time to fill is 12–15 days. If we need it in 5 days, we'll need to move faster on feedback."
> - *Budget:* "For Microsoft 365 and Active Directory skills in this market, candidates are asking $800–$1,000. At $500, we're getting 90% rejections at screening."
> 3. **Give options, not a dead end.**
> - **Option A:** keep the budget and timeline but widen the search: similar titles, adjacent skills, or one step below who can grow.
> - **Option B:** keep the exact profile but adjust the budget or timeline. Even $100 more opens about 30% more candidates.
> - **Option C:** split the role. Hire a strong junior now for the urgent work while we search for the senior.
>
> Then I ask: **"Which trade-off works best for you?"**

*The figures are examples. On the job, quote BruntWork's real numbers.*

### Why does this approach work?

> - I never say "your budget is unrealistic". I show market data.
> - The client stays in control: options, not a no.
> - It protects candidates. I don't bring them to a role that will waste their time.

### Your closing line

> My job is not to say yes to everything. My job is to give the client a clear picture of what their timeline and budget will buy in the real market, so they can decide fast.

## 3. Technical questions: the MAMA framework

### What is the MAMA framework?

> Use it to explain anything technical without jargon:
>
> - **M — Make it human.** Talk about the people affected, not the system.
> - **A — Analogy.** Compare it to something they already know.
> - **M — Make it plain.** No jargon; explain it like you would to someone outside the field.
> - **A — After vs before.** Show the real change: before it was like this, now it's like this.

### What was your greatest challenge? (MAMA answer)

> **Onboarding 80+ health workers onto KenyaEMR in a short time, with no extra hires.**
>
> - **Human:** 80+ nurses and clerks across 50 clinics were affected. They were busy, short-staffed and used to paper.
> - **Analogy:** it was like asking 50 mama mboga shops to drop their notebooks and use a phone app in two weeks.
> - **Plain:** a tight deadline and no extra staff, so I couldn't train everyone myself. I quickly found the right 1–2 fast learners per facility: people others respect, who stay calm when the power goes off. I did short screening calls, then watched them in their first training.
> - **After vs before:** before, staff stayed late writing registers and files got lost. After, 80+ users were onboarded across 15+ sessions, daily use started immediately, and we hit the go-live date.

### Why move from IT to recruitment now?

> I love the people part of IT the most: training, onboarding, helping someone solve a problem. In recruitment I get to do that every day, and my IT background helps because I understand the technical roles I'm hiring for. I can tell if a candidate really knows the work or just wrote it on the CV.

### How will your technical knowledge make you a better recruiter?

> A non-technical recruiter checks keywords. I check if it's real. I can ask, "Walk me through how you fixed a network outage," and know if the answer is fake. I know the tools, like Microsoft 365, Active Directory, SQL and Python, so I won't waste the hiring manager's time with unqualified CVs.

### How would you use automation and the ATS?

> To reduce manual work. I'd use filters and rules in the ATS to tag CVs, send automatic updates so candidates don't ghost, and keep a clean tracker in Sheets so I can see where each role is leaking. Less admin, more time talking to good candidates.

### How does your SLA experience help candidate experience?

> In helpdesk, every ticket needs a fast, clear reply or people get angry. Candidates are the same. I treat them like users: reply fast, tell them where they are in the process, and never leave them silent. My target at PATH was 98% uptime. My target in recruitment is 100% of candidates knowing their status.

### Walk us through how you trained 80+ staff.

> The biggest challenge was the short time. I couldn't train everyone at once, so I found the right fast learners at each facility, the ones others respect. I trained them well with simple one-page guides and WhatsApp support, and they helped train their teams. Recruitment is the same: find the right person fast, onboard them well, and stay close in week 1 so they don't drop off.

### Explain a technical issue to a non-technical manager.

> **Example:** a system went down because the internet box lost power. It's like your home Wi-Fi when KPLC goes. I added a small backup battery, like a power bank for the router, and trained one person to restart it. **Before:** they waited 2 days for me. **After:** back up in 5 minutes.
>
> With hiring managers I do the same: I translate a technical profile into business value, meaning how this skill solves the team's problem now.

## 5. Recruitment cycle and sourcing

### Walk me through the full recruitment cycle.

> **Intake** (must-haves, pay, shift, culture) › **Job ad** (clear on work, pay and shift) › **Sourcing** (applicants, LinkedIn Boolean, referrals) › **Screening** (skills, pay, shift, setup, start date) › **Assessment** (tests) › **Shortlist** (top 2–3 with reasons) › **Client interviews** › **Selection and checks** › **Offer and a clear yes** › **Onboarding and 30/60/90-day check-ins.**
>
> Throughout: the ATS stays updated and privacy is kept.

### Which stage matters most?

> Intake. A vague brief wastes every stage after it. Ten sharp minutes saves weeks.

### Where do hires usually fall apart?

> At the offer, when pay, shift or start date weren't confirmed early. I settle those at screening, so the offer is a formality.

### How do you attract talent, and where would you source IT support?

> I go where they are: LinkedIn, job boards, tech groups on Facebook, Telegram and WhatsApp, and referrals. Strong people know other strong people. For IT support, LinkedIn Boolean search comes first.

### How do you write outreach that doesn't feel like a template?

> Short and personal. One line about something real in their profile, one line on the role, pay and shift, and one clear ask: a 15-minute call.

### How do you fill a hard-to-fill role?

> Widen the net: similar titles, adjacent industries, and people one step below who are ready to move up. Ask the client which must-haves truly are must-haves. Ask strong candidates for referrals.

### How do you sell a role to a passive candidate?

> Find out what they want first: growth, pay, flexibility or better work. Then show honestly how the role gives it. If it doesn't fit, keep the relationship for the next one.

## 6. Screening, candidate experience and offers

### What do you always cover in a screening call?

> Motivation, must-have skills, pay, shift and time zone, internet and power setup, start date, other offers, and contractor status.

### What red flags do you listen for?

> Vague answers about their own work, blaming every past employer, pay or shift far from the role, and not being able to explain a tool they listed.

### How do you check a remote candidate is genuine?

> Camera on, confirm identity, and ask unscripted follow-ups on the CV. If they pause, read, or can't explain their own work, I dig deeper.

### How do you handle a CV gap or an overqualified candidate?

> - **Gap:** ask neutrally and move on. What matters is whether their skills are current and they can do the job now.
> - **Overqualified:** not a problem by itself. Check why they want the role, whether the pay works, and whether they'll stay.

### How do you assess culture fit without bias?

> Define fit as work behaviours: reliability, communication, responsiveness and ownership. Ask everyone the same questions and score against the same criteria. "Someone like me" is not culture fit.

### Which questions shouldn't you ask?

> Age, marital status, pregnancy or children, religion, tribe or ethnicity, health or disability, politics, and in some US states, salary history. Stick to what predicts performance.

### A candidate lied on their CV. What do you do?

> End it politely, note the facts in the ATS, and don't submit them. One fake candidate costs the client's trust.

### What does a good candidate experience look like?

> Fast replies, clear next steps, honest feedback, and no silence.

### A candidate goes silent. What do you do?

> - **Day 1:** email plus WhatsApp or LinkedIn.
> - **Day 2:** a call.
> - **Day 4:** a final note: "I'll close your file on Friday unless I hear from you."
>
> I always keep 2–3 backups warm. Candidates usually ghost because of a slow process, silence from us, or a faster offer elsewhere. Prevention: regular updates, even "no news yet, next update Friday."

### How do you secure commitment, and handle budget gaps, back-outs and counteroffers?

> - **Commitment:** no surprises. Confirm pay and shift at screening, and ask before the offer: "If it comes in at this, will you accept?"
> - **Wants more than the budget:** find out early, not at the offer. If the gap is small, check what's flexible, like a pay review after 90 days or incentives. If it's big, be honest with both sides, and show the client the data if the market is above budget.
> - **Backs out after accepting:** find out why, tell the client immediately with a replacement plan, go to the backups, and fix the cause, usually a counteroffer or a doubt missed at screening.
> - **Counteroffer:** raise it early: "If your employer counters, what will you do?" Remind them why they started looking.

### How do you reject many candidates without it feeling cold?

> Quick and personal: one line of thanks, one line of reason, one line of encouragement. Under 90 seconds each, but never a silent no.

## 7. Clients, metrics, ATS and privacy

### How do you handle an angry client?

> Stay calm and let them finish. Acknowledge the problem, get the facts, agree a fix and a time, and follow up before they chase. They just want to know someone owns it.

### What do you ask in a client intake?

> Must-haves, nice-to-haves and deal-breakers. Pay, shift and time zone. What success looks like at 90 days. Who interviews, and how fast they decide.

### A client keeps rejecting strong candidates without clear reasons.

> Ask for specific feedback on each candidate, compare it against the brief, and look for the pattern. Then recalibrate with real profiles: "Here are three you rejected. What's missing?" Usually the brief changes.

### A client wants to skip steps.

> Respect it, but keep the minimum checks: skills, references and privacy consent. Explain the risk in one sentence and let them decide.

### How do you build trust with a client who's been let down before?

> Small promises, kept fast. A strong shortlist on time, honest updates, and no padding with weak candidates.

### How do you manage targets and volume?

> One tracker, ranked by urgency and how close each role is to filling. I block my shift: sourcing first, screens in the middle, updates and admin at the end. Every role gets a touch every day.

### You're behind target halfway through the month.

> Look at the funnel to find the leak: not enough candidates, too few passing screens, or clients not converting. Fix that stage with more sourcing hours, tighter screening or faster client feedback. Revisit strong runners-up from earlier roles.

### Which metrics matter, and how do you calculate time to fill?

> - **Metrics:** time to fill, submissions per hire, interview-to-offer rate, offer acceptance rate, and how long hires stay. They show where the process is slow or leaking.
> - **Time to fill:** days from the role opening to the offer being accepted. Learn BruntWork's benchmark per role and aim to beat it.

### How do you keep ATS data clean when you're busy?

> Update as I go, not at the end of the day. Every call ends with a stage change and a next step. If it's not in the ATS, it didn't happen.

### Have you used an ATS?

> I've run pipelines in Freshdesk and Zoho Desk: every request logged, tracked by stage and closed on time. An ATS sits in the same family, and I learn fast.

### How do you use AI tools in recruiting?

> To speed up first drafts: job ads, outreach and Boolean ideas. I always check and personalise, and I never paste candidate personal data into public AI tools.

### How do you protect candidate data, and which privacy laws apply?

> - **Protect:** collect only what the role needs, store it in the ATS rather than personal files or chats, share a CV with a client only with the candidate's consent, and delete what's no longer needed. I work with confidential patient data every day, so this is a habit.
> - **Laws:** it depends where the candidate and client are: Kenya's Data Protection Act, the Philippines' Data Privacy Act, GDPR for UK and EU clients. The principles are the same: consent, minimum data, secure storage and limited access.
